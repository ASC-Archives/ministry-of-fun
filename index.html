TheMinistryof Fun

Play is a way of knowing. A building can be a conversation, a machine can get bored, and a song can carry a theory further than a paper. These pages gather two of our starting places, Cedric Price's Fun Palace and Gordon Pask's work on play, and the songs that came out of Ouro Preto.

Starting place one

Cedric Price's Fun Palace

In 1961 the theatre director Joan Littlewood and the architect Cedric Price set out to design a Fun Palace, which they described as a laboratory of fun. It was never meant to be a building in the ordinary sense. It was a framework of steel towers and gantry cranes in which floors, rooms, screens and walkways could be hung, moved and taken down again as the people using it wanted. There was no fixed programme. People would come to dance, to learn, to make things, to argue, to watch science and to do nothing in particular, and the structure would rearrange itself around what they actually did.

The project had no conventional client. Littlewood acted as its director, and committees worked on particular aspects of the design. Around 1963 Gordon Pask joined at the invitation of Littlewood and Price and organised the Fun Palace Cybernetics Committee. Price later recalled that Pask accepted the post unpaid, and that it was Pask who settled, after weeks of indecision, on a single high-level gantry crane running the whole length of the building. His advice was that if one crane was not enough, the answer was a second Fun Palace.

By 1964 the project was about to go public, with a site found at Mill Meads in the Lea Valley in East London and a fund-raising committee that included Buckminster Fuller and Yehudi Menuhin. It was never built. Its influence lasted all the same: many cite it as an inspiration for the Pompidou Centre in Paris, and since 2014 the Fun Palaces campaign has carried Littlewood's idea into libraries, museums and streets each October.

For a cybernetician the Fun Palace is interesting less as architecture than as a proposition: that a building could be a regulator in conversation with its users, learning from what they do rather than telling them what to do.

Starting place two

Gordon Pask and play

Gordon Pask (1928–1996) called himself a philosopher mechanic, and much of his work began as play. He built machines that responded to people, and he found that the interesting ones were those that did not simply obey. A machine that always does what you expect is a tool. A machine that notices, adapts, and sometimes loses interest becomes a partner, and the pleasure of using it comes from the conversation between you.

That idea runs from his early entertainment machines, through his work for the Fun Palace and for architecture, to Conversation Theory, where learning is understood as two participants coming to share concepts that each can teach back to the other. Play, for Pask, was not a break from serious inquiry. It was one of the clearest places to watch learning happen.

An aesthetically potent environment

Writing about Musicolour and his later work, Pask set out what an environment needs if people are to find it rewarding over time. In paraphrase:

  1. It offers enough variety that there is always more to find.
  2. It contains forms a person can learn to interpret and come to recognise.
  3. It gives cues that guide that learning without dictating it.
  4. It responds to the person, so that the two can enter a conversation.

Rebuilt, fifty years on

Paul Pangaro and the Colloquy of Mobiles

Pask's Colloquy of Mobiles was shown at Cybernetic Serendipity at the ICA in London in 1968: larger-than-life figures hanging from the ceiling that turned, blinked and called to one another, courting through light and sound, and that visitors could join with mirrors and torches. It was dismantled after the exhibition, and for half a century it survived only in photographs, drawings and memory.

For its fiftieth anniversary in 2018, Paul Pangaro, then chair of the Masters programme in Interaction Design at the College for Creative Studies in Detroit, set out with the master fabricator TJ McLeish and their students to rebuild it at full scale. McLeish drew up design plans from the historical record. The replica keeps the 1968 form as closely as possible, including the three female mobiles first designed by Yolanda Sonnabend, while using modern software, sensors and motors to recreate the behaviours Pask designed. Fresh details came from Mark Dowson, who built the original electronics; no recording of the original sounds is known to exist.

The replica then crossed the Atlantic. In February 2020 it opened at the Centre Pompidou in Paris, in Mutations / Créations 4: Neurones, les intelligences simulées, an exhibition tracing a joined history of brains, neural networks, artificial intelligence and cybernetic artefacts. A symposium alongside it included a panel on the dead ends and the future of cybernetics, with Pangaro, Andrew Pickering and Margit Rosen of ZKM. In 2021 the Colloquy entered the permanent collection of ZKM in Karlsruhe.

There is a loop here the Ministry cannot resist. The Pompidou is the building many trace back to the Fun Palace, and Pask was the Fun Palace's cybernetician. Half a century on, his conversing machines were finally shown in the building that had grown from the Palace's idea.

Try it

A small Musicolour

Play the four pads, or the keys A, S, D and F. The machine answers with light. Repeat yourself and it will get bored, dim, and wait for something it has not heard. It is a toy, not a reconstruction, but the principle is Pask's.
